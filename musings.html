New Directions (07/29/2026) Nearly 3 years went by. Anyway, let's pick up where we left off. I need to make this site have better mobile compatibility and a truly mind boggling amount of class notes to catch up on.

Long story short: I graduated, and then graduated again (B.S. Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering, M.S. Chemical Engineering at NYU), got a job at Leidos doing some government adjacent work (mostly chasing subcontractors around), and now I'm quitting. My last day is August 20th, 2026. What's next?

Over my year of working, I've gotten deeply interested in automation in the service of scientific discovery. I've generally been AI averse in the past, as I didn't want to use it as a mental crutch. But in my opinion it seems more and more likely to be the future of science. We have vast databases of scientific articles where doing a traditional literature review would be nearly impossible, while AI can (with some hallucinations, of course) synthesize immense quantities of information, helping us find research gaps/opportunities for experimental revalidation. And complex AI/ML algorithms have slashed the computational cost of creating high-fidelity quantum chemical models.

But what I've mentioned so far isn't really automation, and that's where I think the gap is. In doing actual chemistry, we needed people to actually run the experiments. To be fair, a vast amount of modern science has been about creating the tools that allow for greater degrees of experimental automation (autopipettes, PCR thermocyclers, robotic arms, etc.). Now, with the advent of better AI tools and advanced robotics, we can start to think about actually trying to close the loop here, especially in the realm of experiments that are "high-throughput". What do I mean by close the loop? Picture this: you are studying various different metal organic frameworks (MOFs) with a particular focus, let's say carbon dioxide capture. We've synthesized and published plenty of articles about loads of different MOFs, and we also have quantum chemical modeling that lets us create hypothetical MOFs that would then need to be synthesized. My task: create (or find) a MOF that separates CO2 from a particular flue gas with a particular efficiency at a certain temperature. I can make a value function based off my desired MOF performance. Now, AI steps in. It reviews all the existing literature, cataloguing all the various MOFs. It runs pseudo quantum-chemical simulations of them, along with other hypothetical MOF structures. By pseudo I mean the AI isn't actually running a computationally intensive calculation with Hamiltonians - many calculations have already been done and its making an educated guess based off of them. It finds candidates that are in theory well suited. AI plans out synthesis routes for the ones that do not exist yet, and (this part is still human) a lab worker actually procures the materials and performs the synthesis of the MOFs. The MOFs are then analyzed for their separation efficiency for some flue gas at some temperature, and AI runs the data analysis, leverages our value function, and determines the best MOF for the job.

I want to build these automated systems that can run experiments quickly and give the AI plenty of data to analyze as (in my opinion) this will accelerate scientific progress. If I create a system that can vary flue gas composition and temperature, I can use Gaussian Process Regression to create an effectiveness "heat map" for each MOF - more data that'll help improve prediction fidelity and maybe give some insight into the fundamental chemical mechanisms guiding the performance.
